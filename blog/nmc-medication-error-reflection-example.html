NMC Medication Error Reflection Example: Worked Template

Example Published 18 August 2026

A medication error is one of the most common things nurses write a reflective account about. And for good reason: it's a clear, real event with an obvious lesson. The challenge is writing it up so it actually demonstrates reflection, not just describes what happened.

Here's a worked example you can adapt, followed by a fill-in template and the mistakes to avoid.

The worked example: a wrong-dose medication error

Reflective account (adapted, 290 words)

During a busy night shift I was administering controlled drugs to two patients in adjacent bays. I had already double-checked the dose for one patient when I was interrupted by a call bell. When I returned, I prepared the second patient's medication from memory instead of rechecking the chart. I gave 5 mg of a drug where 2.5 mg was prescribed.

I realised the error immediately after administration, when I checked the paperwork to sign. I informed the nurse in charge, contacted the doctor, and completed a Datix incident report. I explained to the patient and their family what had happened, apologised, and monitored the patient closely. No harm resulted, but it could easily have done.

Reflecting on it, I had let time pressure override a safety habit I usually follow without thinking. The interruption broke my concentration and I did not re-establish my checks before acting.

What I changed: I now refuse to be drawn away mid-preparation. I finish the check I have started or start it again from scratch before leaving the area. I also spoke to my manager about moving the medication preparation area to reduce interruptions, and this is now being reviewed on the ward.

This links to the NMC Code, theme 3, Preserve Safety. I prioritised speed over a repeat check and put the patient at avoidable risk. I will audit my practice over the next month to confirm the new habit is holding, and I have added a reminder to recheck the chart before every administration, even for drugs I think I remember.

Why this works (and how to replicate it)

The example hits all five elements the NMC looks for, in order. It states what happened, then the learning, then the change, then the Code link, then the next step. It's honest about the pressure but doesn't make excuses. It names the exact error (wrong dose) and the exact change (finish or restart the check).

Notice what it does not do: it doesn't write an essay about the theory of medication safety. It stays specific to one event. Assessors see hundreds of vague accounts; a precise one stands out.

Fill-in template

Copy and adapt this structure

1. What happened (the context and the error)
Describe the shift, the task, and the specific error. Include the drug and dose if you can. What interrupted you or contributed?

2. What you did at the time
Who did you tell? What steps did you take immediately (incident report, monitoring, telling the patient)?

3. What you learned
Name the root cause honestly: time pressure, distraction, a knowledge gap, a system problem.

4. How you changed your practice
One concrete, specific change. Not "I will be more careful." Something a colleague could observe.

5. Link to the NMC Code
Name the theme (almost always Preserve Safety for medication errors) and say why it applies.

6. What you'll do next
A short follow-up: audit your practice, do a learning module, ask for supervision.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Describing but not reflecting. A timeline of events is not reflection. The learning and the change are what earn the marks.
  • Blaming the system only. "The pharmacy mislabelled it" without any "and here's what I now check" reads as defensive.
  • Vague changes. "I will be more careful" is a red flag to assessors. Be specific and observable.
  • Listing all four themes. One theme, well explained, beats four themes mentioned in passing.

Important: reflection is not incident reporting

A reflective account does not replace your duty to report a medication error through your employer's formal process or your duty of candour to the patient. Do both. The reflective account is your private record of learning, kept for your revalidation.
