What Your NMC Confirmer Conversation Actually Looks Like

The questions your confirmer will ask, how to prepare without over-preparing, and why the conversation is a safety net, not a hurdle.

You have picked your confirmer, and now it is the day of the meeting. If your stomach is doing loops, you are not alone. The confirmer conversation is the part of revalidation most nurses quietly dread, not because it is difficult, but because nobody tells you what it actually involves. Here is the honest version.

It is not an interview

The word "confirmer" makes the whole thing sound far more formal than it is. Your confirmer is not there to interrogate you or catch you out. Their job is to confirm that you have met the revalidation requirements, and to sign off that your evidence is a true and accurate reflection of what you discussed. That is all they are being asked to do.

The NMC recommends you speak to your confirmer face to face where you can, so you can talk them through how you have met each requirement. Most conversations happen in a quiet room at work or over a video call, and they usually last twenty to forty minutes. The tone is a conversation between colleagues, not a panel hearing.

What your confirmer will ask

Your confirmer is confirming that you have met all of the revalidation requirements, so expect the conversation to touch on each one: your practice hours, your CPD, your reflective accounts, your feedback, your reflective discussion, your health and character declarations, and your indemnity cover.

In practice, most of the talking happens around a few of these. They will ask how many hours you have worked and whether your record is a fair reflection of it. They will ask about your CPD, often simply "what did you do and what did you learn from it." They will read your reflective accounts and ask you to talk through one or two of them. And they will want a sense of whether you have collected feedback from patients or colleagues.

None of this requires a perfect answer. It requires honesty.

The important part: they confirm, they do not judge

This is the thing that relaxes people once they understand it. Your confirmer is not marking your work or deciding whether you pass. They are there to confirm that what you have told the NMC is a true and accurate summary of a discussion you genuinely had. If they spot something inconsistent, or a reflective account that does not seem to match what you have described, they will flag it so you can fix it before you submit.

That is a favour, not a problem.

How to prepare without over-preparing

You do not need slides. You need your revalidation evidence in front of you: your practice hours log, your CPD record, your reflective accounts, and your feedback. Have them printed or open on a screen so you can point to them.

Skim your reflective accounts the night before so the details are fresh. That is enough. The conversation that matters is the one you have honestly, not one you have rehearsed.

One small but worthwhile habit: as you go through the year, add a one-line note to each reflective account about a question someone might ask. It makes this conversation effortless when it arrives.

What good looks like

A good confirmer conversation ends with you knowing that what you will submit is accurate, and with a couple of small improvements you would not have spotted on your own. You walk away with the signature done, not with a list of corrections.

That is the whole point of the step. It is a safety net, not a hurdle.

Make the conversation the easy part

The reason nurses dread the confirmer conversation is not the conversation itself. It is the fear of not being able to find their evidence when the questions start. Scrolling through old emails, hunting down certificates, and trying to remember how many hours you worked last year turns a twenty-minute chat into a week of stress.
